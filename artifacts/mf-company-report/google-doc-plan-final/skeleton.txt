Executive Summary
MF is an industrial-engineering services company, not merely an architecture studio or construction contractor. It sells the reduction of risk in capital projects: defining the right facility, coordinating technical disciplines, protecting cost and schedule, supervising execution and—when contracted—delivering construction or turnkey responsibility.
Its public operating model has three commercial pillars: Architecture & Engineering, Construction Management/Inspection, and Construction. A client can buy one piece, combine design with owner's-engineer oversight, or place broader responsibility with MF.
The “15 internal disciplines” combine different kinds of work. Five are project governance and control functions, four establish the site's/building's physical base, and six design the utilities and operating systems. Their competitive advantage depends less on any single discipline than on coordinating decisions across all 15.
The central operating challenge is information continuity. A production premise becomes architectural space, structural loads, electrical capacity, HVAC demand, piping routes, automation logic, cost and schedule. If one change fails to reach one discipline, the error can survive until procurement or construction, where correction is expensive.

What it is not
MF does not manufacture factory equipment and is not simply a labor contractor. It sits between the client's business/operations team, architects and engineers, equipment vendors, construction contractors, regulators, certification partners and the final operating plant. Its product is a technically coordinated, buildable and governable capital project.
MF can occupy four different positions in the same kind of project
The contract determines whether MF is a designer, the owner's technical representative, the builder, or an integrated combination. This matters in the meeting because each position has different workflows, documents, permissions and liability.
Public portfolio pages show many engagements where MF designed and managed/inspected while another company performed construction. Its website also markets an internal construction unit and full turnkey delivery. Therefore, do not assume every MF project uses the same contract model—or that “project,” “management,” “inspection” and “construction” are interchangeable.

The operating model is a chain of decisions, not a chain of drawings
A successful MF project progressively converts a business requirement into an operating industrial asset. The exact sequence varies by contract and project type, but the public workflow consistently points to ten stages.
The most important concept is the design basis or set of premises. A statement such as “the line must produce 80,000 bottles per hour” affects area, foundations, equipment, electrical demand, cooling, water, piping, controls, logistics, cost and schedule. MF itself identifies undefined or outdated premises and weak discipline integration as causes of expensive dimensioning errors.
MF describes three review moments: when premises are closed with operations, at the end of basic design, and before executive design is released to construction. Those gates are cheap opportunities to catch errors before they become concrete, steel or purchased equipment.

MF's list mixes management functions, design coordination and technical trades. Treating all 15 as equivalent departments hides how the company actually produces work.
Project governance and controls determine whether the project should proceed, how it is contracted, how it is scheduled, how quality is controlled and how models are coordinated.
The physical base model defines the site, building, foundations and structural frame.
Plant and building systems make the facility operate: water, drainage, climate, power, process transport, controls and fire safety.

Coordination—not headcount—is the value of having the disciplines inside MF
The six plant/building-system disciplines depend on decisions made by the physical-base teams, while every team depends on project controls. For example, MF explicitly says hydraulic design begins from Architecture, Civil and Steel models. Electrical capacity depends on loads from production equipment, HVAC, pumps and automation. Concrete foundations depend on mechanical equipment loads. BIM is the layer that exposes these interfaces before the site does.
So “15 internal disciplines” is a commercial promise of reduced interface risk. The meeting should test whether MF's actual information systems let it realize that promise consistently across projects, offices, suppliers and construction sites.

Three examples show how work crosses the disciplines
Example 1 — A production-capacity increase
If a client increases required throughput, Mechanical/Piping and equipment data change first. That can increase electrical load, cooling demand, water demand, structural loads, space, fire risk, cost and schedule. Planning must reforecast; architecture and structures may need redesign; automation I/O changes; BIM must re-coordinate; quality must ensure the approved change reaches released documents.
Example 2 — A brownfield expansion
MF must first understand the existing plant through drawings, surveys, scanning and interviews with operations/maintenance. Infrastructure, architecture and structures determine what can physically fit. Systems teams determine tie-ins to live power, water, drainage, HVAC, piping and controls. Planning must fit critical connections into shutdown windows, while site management separates construction logistics from ongoing production.
Example 3 — A late supplier change
A vendor's revised equipment footprint or utility requirement can alter foundations, access clearances, cable routes, pipe connections, HVAC loads and delivery dates. The change must move from supplier approval into every affected model, drawing, procurement record, schedule and field instruction. If any artifact remains stale, the site can build the wrong condition.
These examples explain why MF repeatedly uses the words integração, compatibilização, rastreabilidade and previsibilidade.
Greenfield, brownfield and retrofit require different operating behavior
Greenfield: a new facility on a new site. The early decisions are geotechnical conditions, site preparation, master planning, utilities, logistics, scalability and the complete design basis.
Brownfield expansion: new capacity inside or adjacent to an operating plant. Existing information may be incomplete; space is constrained; tie-ins and shutdowns must be planned; construction cannot disrupt production.
Retrofit: modernization of existing systems, structures or processes. The team must understand what exists, preserve safe operation, meet current standards and integrate new technology with old assets.
Technology/plant innovation: changes focused on process, automation, energy performance or Industry 4.0 rather than only building area.

Brownfield is generally the most information-sensitive case because the “current truth” is split across old drawings, field conditions, maintenance knowledge, supplier records and the memories of experienced employees.
BIM is MF's coordination method, but the public tool picture is incomplete
MF states that projects are modeled in Revit 3D and documented in DWG 2D. It presents BIM as a shared model for discipline integration, clash reduction, schedule/cost linkage and lifecycle information.
3D: coordinated geometry and clash visibility.
4D: the 3D model connected to construction sequence and schedule; recent MF material describes using it to validate execution strategy and identify risks before field work.
5D and beyond: MF marketing discusses cost, quantities and wider lifecycle/sustainability dimensions, but the exact production implementation is not publicly documented.
Construction control: MF discusses Curva S, Curva ABC, value engineering, planned-versus-actual analysis and formal change control.
Reality capture: its brownfield material references surveys and scanning; the exact hardware/software stack is unknown.
Commissioning: public material describes validation, inspections, updated documentation and structured handover; data-center content specifically mentions FAT, SAT and integrated-system testing.

Unknowns that matter: Revit version, common data environment, model federation/clash software, document-management platform, naming/version rules, approval workflow, source of truth, use of Primavera or other schedulers, and how field changes reach as-built records.

What this means for your meeting
The most credible way to speak to MF is to frame Urso as infrastructure for project continuity across disciplines. Their business promise is predictability. Their operational exposure is that a decision can be correct in one document, model or person's head and still fail to reach every dependent discipline.
You should understand these distinctions before proposing anything:
“Engineering” is not one team; it is a coordinated network of design and control functions.
A project's truth changes over time: premise, basic design, executive design, approved change and as-built state are not interchangeable.
The same client may require strict project isolation because designs, costs and processes are confidential.
Senior-engineer knowledge is valuable because historical projects shape today's judgment.
BIM solves geometric coordination; it does not automatically govern meeting decisions, approvals, standards, emails, lessons learned or who may access them.
The first Urso pilot should target one live information flow—such as approved premises and change propagation—not “AI for all 15 disciplines.”

A meeting-ready explanation
MF takes an industrial investment from feasibility to an operating asset. Its 15 internal disciplines turn business premises into coordinated architecture, structures, utilities, controls, construction and commissioning. The company makes money by designing that system, governing its execution, and sometimes constructing it. Its advantage is integration; its risk is any break in the information chain between those disciplines.

Evidence boundaries and caveats
This briefing was checked on August 1, 2026, primarily against MF's own website, official LinkedIn page, individual project records and code of conduct.
Official pages are authoritative for how MF presents its services, but much of the prose is marketing content rather than an internal operating manual. The lifecycle and handoff model is a synthesis from MF's published workflow plus standard industrial-engineering dependencies.
Public sources do not disclose the internal org chart, discipline staffing, financial performance, project margins, detailed software architecture, document repositories, approval matrices or actual delay data.
Company scale is inconsistent across public materials: older notes and pages were stale; current LinkedIn says 201–500 employees, shows 154 associated profiles, and MF's anniversary post says more than 200 professionals.
The main institutional website names Franz Fuchs and Salvator Minerbo as founders; MF's official anniversary post also names Mário Philippsen. Keep the fuller three-founder version while recognizing the website discrepancy.
MF's stated vision of R$100M annual revenue is a target, not evidence that current revenue has reached that amount.
Claims about current customer engagements should be limited to what individual project pages or current official posts establish.
